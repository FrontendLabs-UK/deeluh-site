Letter of administration in Lagos

When someone dies without a will, the people entitled to deal with the estate have to be appointed by the court before a bank, a buyer or a land registry will deal with them. In Lagos that appointment is a letter of administration, granted by the Probate Registry. This page lists what you have to gather, what happens after you file, and how long each part takes.

Last checked 23 November 2026. This is general information, not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for a lawyer. What happens after you file

This part is a sequence, and each step waits on the one before it. The waiting is the reason the whole thing takes months rather than weeks.

  1. You file at Ikeja or Lagos Those are the two divisions that handle probate. Lagos also has an online probate portal through the High Court, so ask whether your application can start there.
  2. The Registrar interviews the administrators and any sureties To check that the people applying are entitled to, and that the sureties are good for the bond.
  3. The Registry publishes a notice, and anyone who objects has 21 days Published guidance says estates of ₦100,000 or less are exempt from this step. If an objection comes in, the application stops being a paperwork exercise and becomes a contested matter — that is the point to have a lawyer if you do not already.
  4. If nobody objects, the letter is drafted and granted One Lagos firm puts the whole thing at roughly four to six months from filing to grant. Treat that as an estimate from practice, not a service standard the Registry has published.

What each paper is, and why the Registry asks for it

The list above is short, but three of the items are not documents you already own — they are things you create at the Registry, and knowing what each one is for makes the interview much easier.

The application letter

This is the one you write. It is addressed to the Probate Registrar and it says who died, when, that they left no will, who is applying and why they are entitled to. It is not a form, so nobody hands it to you; it opens the file, and the forms follow it.

The oath of administration

A sworn statement by the administrators that they will collect the estate, pay what the estate owes, and distribute the rest according to law rather than preference. The oath is why the grant carries weight with a bank: the people holding it have accepted a duty, not just a title.

The administration bond, and the sureties behind it

The bond is a promise, in a set sum, that the administrators will do the job properly. The Registrar may require sureties — other people who stand behind that promise — and will want to satisfy himself that they are good for it. This is the item that most often sends people home for another appointment, because a surety has to be found, be willing, and be able to show they qualify. Ask what your estate requires before the interview rather than on the day.

The affidavit that nobody has applied before

Estates attract more than one set of relatives. The affidavit, sworn by one of the two administrators, tells the Registry that this is the first application for this estate — so if somebody else has already started, the Registry finds out now rather than after a grant has issued.

Who applies, and who does not

Letters of administration are for an estate with no will. If there is a will, the executors named in it apply for probate instead, which is a different process with different papers. If there is a will but no surviving executor, that is a third situation again — take advice rather than assuming this page applies.

Published guidance is consistent that the Registry expects at least two applicants, drawn from the people entitled to the estate. A single applicant is the most common reason an application stalls at the first hurdle. Who is entitled depends on the deceased’s circumstances, including whether their marriage and estate fall under customary law — which is a question for a lawyer and not one this page can answer for you.

Where people get stuck

  • Applying alone. One applicant is turned away. Find the second person before you file, not after.
  • Assuming the bank will wait. A bank will not release an account to relatives without a grant, and asking repeatedly does not change that. The grant is the thing that moves it.
  • Paying people who are not the Registry. A Lagos practitioner has written about unofficial demands from local government officials, which are not Registry fees. If somebody asks you for money, ask what the official receipt will say.
  • Forgetting the 21 days. The notice period is not a formality you can shorten. Plan for it, and do not promise a buyer or a beneficiary a date that assumes it away.
  • Treating an objection as paperwork. If somebody objects inside the 21 days, the application stops being an administrative exercise. That is the point to have a lawyer, if you do not have one already.

What it costs

Being confirmed — we are not printing a number we cannot stand behind

The published figures contradict each other. One guide puts the cost at 5–10% of the value of the estate, which we could not verify for Lagos. A Lagos practitioner writes separately that local government officials sometimes ask for an unofficial ₦3,000–₦5,000, which is not a Registry fee at all. We have asked the Lagos Probate Registry for its current fee schedule and will publish that, with the date we read it, instead of an average of other people’s guesses.
